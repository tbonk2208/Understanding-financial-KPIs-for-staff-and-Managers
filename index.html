The three statements: profit, position, cash

Every KPI is built from three reports. Know what each one answers and most indicators explain themselves.

Net income was $ million, yet operations produced $ million of cash. Profit and cash are different things: depreciation, amortisation and the loss on the sale reduced profit without any cash leaving in that year.

How the three statements feed each other

  1. Net income is the first line of the cash flow statement.
  2. Sales that are not yet paid become receivables.
  3. Net income is added to equity.
  4. A rise in inventory and receivables is deducted on the way from profit to cash.
  5. The change in cash lands in the cash balance.

Follow one sale: it is profit when shipped, a receivable until paid, and cash only then. The same holds for a purchase (inventory first, cost of sales later) and for a machine (cash out today, depreciation for years).

How the three connect

Net income flows into equity. Sales that are not yet paid sit in receivables. Purchases not yet sold sit in inventories. The cash flow statement reconciles profit with the change in cash by undoing these timing differences.

In your role: operations & supply chain

You mostly act on cost of sales (scrap, yield, labour hours, freight) and on inventories. A pallet in the warehouse is on the balance sheet, not in the income statement, until it ships. That is why building stock can make a month's profit look better while cash gets worse.

In your role: sales & marketing

You act on the top line and on receivables. An order becomes sales only on delivery, and it becomes cash only when the customer pays. Payment terms you agree are a balance-sheet decision.

In your role: R&D & engineering

Your salaries and project costs are mostly expensed in SG&A as they occur. Equipment you request is capital expenditure: it lands on the balance sheet and is depreciated over years.

In your role: managers

Your budget is a slice of the income statement, but you are also accountable for the balance-sheet items your decisions create: stock levels, customer terms, capital requests. Ask for all three views of your unit, not only the P&L.

Module 2

Sales growth: organic, acquired or currency?

"Sales up " says little until you know where the growth came from.

Third-quarter 2026 sales were $ million, up from $ million. Growth has three sources of very different quality:

  • Organic: more volume, better prices or a richer product mix in businesses already owned. This shows competitive strength.
  • Acquisitions: sales bought with capital. They cost money and must earn it back.
  • Currency translation: foreign sales converted at a different exchange rate. Nobody sold more.

Segment A, Q3 2026

Of the segment's growth, a little more than half was organic. In Segment B the whole was organic, driven by a recovery in its main end market.

What drives organic growth

Sales change ≈ volume effect + price effect + mix effect

Price is the most powerful of the three because a price increase has almost no cost attached (Module 3). Mix means selling relatively more of the products with higher prices or margins. The report names stronger demand in several end markets and pricing actions as drivers, with weaker demand in one region working against them.

How to read it

  • Always split it first. Total growth of 13.6% and organic growth of 7.5% describe two different things: what the group bought and what the business earned.
  • Compare with the market. Growing 5% in a market that grows 10% is a loss of share. Growing 2% in a shrinking market is a gain.
  • Check the base. Strong growth after a weak prior-year quarter is partly recovery. Look at two or three years together.
  • Ask what it cost. Growth says nothing about profit. Growth won by discounts, long payment terms or extra stock shows up later in margin and cash.
  • One quarter is not a trend. Large orders and delivery timing shift sales between quarters. Order intake tells you more about direction.

Organic growth only counts when it ships. On-time delivery, lead time and capacity decide whether an order book becomes sales this quarter or next. Late deliveries shift sales, and in aftermarket business they can lose them.

In your role: sales & marketing

Separate your own growth into volume, price and mix before you celebrate it. Ten per cent from price is worth far more profit than ten per cent from discount-driven volume. Report all three for your territory.

In your role: R&D & engineering

New products feed organic growth and, more importantly, mix: differentiated products carry higher prices. A useful personal KPI is the share of sales from products launched in the last few years.

In your role: managers

When comparing your unit with the group, compare organic with organic. Acquired growth at group level is not a benchmark for a site that has not acquired anything.

Operating leverage: why margins rise when sales rise

Sales grew , but SG&A grew only . Costs that do not rise in step with sales (buildings, management, systems, part of the workforce) are spread over more dollars of sales. As a result, SG&A fell from to of sales and the operating margin rose by about .

The same mechanism works in reverse. When sales fall, fixed costs stay and margins drop faster than sales. This is why Segment B's margin moved by four percentage points within a year as demand in its main end market recovered.

Why price matters most

Try it: a small change on $ million of annual sales illustration

Assumptions: sales at the midpoint of 2026 guidance; a price change falls fully to profit; extra volume earns the Q3 2026 gross margin (which understates the effect, because part of cost of sales is fixed). Real effects depend on product, customer and capacity.

How to read it

  • A margin change has five possible causes: price, input cost, mix, utilisation of fixed cost, and waste. Before judging a change, find out which. They call for different actions.
  • Percentage and amount can move in opposite directions. An order at a below-average margin lowers the percentage and still adds profit if capacity is free. Steering only by the percentage rejects good business; steering only by the amount fills the plant with poor business.
  • Gross margin isolates the product. If gross margin is stable and operating margin falls, the cause is in SG&A. If gross margin falls, look at price, material, mix and the plant.
  • Rising margins on rising sales are partly automatic. That is operating leverage, not necessarily better management. The test is what happens when sales stop growing.
  • Compare only like with like. A distribution business and an engineered-products business have different normal margins. Compare a business with its own history and direct peers.

Module 4

EBITDA: operating profit before depreciation

The headline profit number in the example group's reports, as in those of many listed companies. Understand what is added back, and what the figure therefore cannot show.

EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. It approximates the cash earning power of operations, independent of how the company is financed, where it is taxed and how much it paid for past investments and acquisitions.

In this tutorial it is calculated as operating income plus depreciation and amortisation. Everything below operating income (interest, tax, and non-operating items such as the loss on the sale of a business) is outside it. Some companies start from net income and add back only interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation; the two routes give the same figure only when there are no non-operating items.

From net income to EBITDA, full-year 2025

Net income of $ million becomes EBITDA of $ million. Tax, interest and the non-operating items lead back to operating income; adding depreciation and amortisation, the largest single step, gives EBITDA.

How to read it critically

  • Depreciation is a real cost. Machines wear out and must be replaced. EBITDA ignores this, so always look at capital expenditure and free cash flow as well (Module 6).
  • Amortisation of acquired intangibles is large here because the company grew by acquisition. It is non-cash today, but the cash was spent when the business was bought (Module 9).
  • One-time items inside operating cost stay in. A restructuring or a large warranty case lowers EBITDA in the period it is booked. When comparing periods, check whether such an item explains the change.
  • Its strength: it lets you compare periods and businesses without distortion from the sale of a business or a refinancing.

How to read it

  • EBITDA is not cash. It ignores capital expenditure, working capital, interest and tax. A business can report strong EBITDA and still consume cash. Read it together with free cash flow.
  • Look at the size of the gap to net income. Here EBITDA is several times net income. A large gap means much of the picture sits in what EBITDA leaves out: depreciation, amortisation, interest, tax and non-operating results.
  • Check for unusual items inside it. A restructuring charge, a legal settlement or a gain on selling a building sits in operating income and moves EBITDA for one period. Ask whether the change you see will repeat.
  • Capital-heavy and capital-light businesses are not comparable on EBITDA. The same EBITDA margin is worth less where machines must be replaced constantly.
  • Use it for what it is good at: comparing the operating performance of one business over time, without distortion from financing and one-off events.

In your role: operations & supply chain

Everything you do to cost of sales shows up in EBITDA directly. Depreciation of your machines does not, so EBITDA does not reward running equipment longer or penalise a new line. Watch that this does not hide the true cost of capital-heavy solutions.

In your role: sales & marketing

EBITDA margin is the reason "profitable growth" is not the same as growth. An order at a margin far below the segment's average raises sales and lowers the margin percentage at the same time.

In your role: R&D & engineering

Development spending reduces EBITDA today for sales in later years. The releases repeatedly mention "growth investments" holding margins back; your project is one of these, so be ready to say when and how it pays back.

In your role: managers

When you explain a change in EBITDA, separate what will repeat from what will not. And never judge an investment on EBITDA alone: it ignores the capital it consumes.

Mix: the average can move when nothing inside changes

Segment A earns a higher margin than Segment B. If Segment B grows faster, the company's average margin is pulled toward Segment B's level even if each segment holds its own margin. The same happens inside every business between product lines, customers and regions. Always ask whether a margin change is a real change in performance or a change in mix.

How to read it

  • Read level and change separately. Segment A's 28% is a high, stable level. Segment B's move from 18% to 22% is a change, driven by volume returning. Stability and momentum are different qualities.
  • A segment margin is before corporate cost, depreciation and amortisation. It measures what the segment's managers control. It overstates what the segment finally contributes to net income.
  • Check mix before concluding anything from the group margin. If the lower-margin segment grows faster, the group margin can fall while both segments improve.
  • High margin often signals pricing power, for example critical parts that are a small share of the customer's cost. Such margins are defended by quality and delivery, not by cost cutting alone.
  • Volatile margins signal high fixed cost or a cyclical market. They need a cautious view of "normal" earnings.

How to read it

  • Rising DSO has three very different causes: more sales late in the quarter (harmless), longer agreed terms (a commercial decision), or customers paying late (a credit risk). The ratio alone cannot tell you which; the overdue list can.
  • Rising DIO can be preparation or a problem. Stock built for confirmed orders or a launch is planned. Stock that grows while sales are flat points to forecast errors, large batches or products nobody wants.
  • High DPO is not automatically good. It finances the business, but it can mean lost early-payment discounts and strained suppliers. A sudden rise can also be a sign of cash shortage.
  • Working capital grows with sales. Judge it in days or as a percentage of sales, not in dollars. More dollars at constant days is normal growth.
  • Free cash flow is lumpy. One large project or one week of payment timing moves a quarter. Compare several years with net income: over time most of the profit should arrive as cash.
  • Year-end figures flatter. Many companies collect hard and delay payments before the balance sheet date. Averages over the year are more honest.

Module 7

Leverage and capital allocation

How much debt the company carries, and what it does with the cash it generates.

Net leverage

Net leverage = (total debt − cash) ÷ EBITDA of the last twelve months

At 30 September 2026: ($ − $) ÷ $ million = , which matches the reported . The trailing-twelve-month EBITDA is own calculation (nine months 2026 plus fourth quarter 2025).

Read it as: with all operating earnings and no other use of cash, net debt could be repaid in about years. At the end of 2025, after the acquisitions of that year, it stood at . Since then $ million of revolving debt has been repaid.

Why it matters to everyone: low leverage means lower interest cost, resilience in a downturn, and capacity to invest or acquire. High leverage means lenders' conditions start to limit decisions.

Where the cash goes

A company can use cash in five ways: invest in its own operations (capital expenditure), buy businesses, repay debt, pay dividends, or buy back shares. In 2025 acquisitions were the largest use. In 2026 so far it has been debt repayment. The quarterly dividend is per share, and the board has authorised up to $ million of share repurchases.

How to read it

  • The ratio has two parts. Leverage falls when debt is repaid or when EBITDA rises. A fall caused only by peak earnings in a cyclical business reverses when earnings normalise.
  • Rules of thumb, not laws: for industrial companies, below about 2× is usually seen as comfortable, around 3× as stretched, above 4× as constraining. Lenders' agreements set the real limits.
  • Low leverage is an option, not just safety. It means the company can act when an acquisition or an investment opportunity appears, and can keep investing through a downturn.
  • Very low leverage has a cost too. Equity is more expensive than debt, so unused borrowing capacity lowers the return for owners.
  • Capital allocation reveals priorities. Where the cash went (own investment, acquisitions, debt, dividends, buybacks) tells you more about strategy than any presentation.

From EBITDA to EPS

EBITDA of $ million is per share; EPS is . In between lie depreciation and amortisation ($ million), interest ($ million) and tax ($ million).

For a company that has grown by acquisition, amortisation is a large part of this. Other intangible assets fell from $ million to $ million in nine months, which suggests amortisation of roughly $20 million per quarter own estimate. It lowers EPS every quarter although no cash leaves, which is why EPS understates the cash the business generates.

These costs change little from quarter to quarter. A rise in operating profit therefore arrives in net income almost in full: EBITDA rose by about 25% against the prior-year quarter, EPS by about 65%.

How EPS grows

  • Higher operating profit (Modules 2 to 5)
  • Lower interest through debt repayment (Module 7)
  • A lower tax rate
  • Fewer shares through buybacks

Only the first is created in day-to-day operations, and it is by far the largest in a growing company.

How to read it

  • Know what is inside it. Unlike EBITDA, EPS carries everything: depreciation, amortisation from acquisitions, interest, tax and one-time gains or losses. A single disposal can cut it sharply for a year, as the 2025 figure shows.
  • Ask where EPS growth came from. Growth from operating profit is repeatable. Growth from fewer shares, a lower tax rate or lower interest is one-time or financial.
  • EPS ignores the capital used. A company can raise EPS through acquisitions that earn less than they cost. Read EPS with return on capital.
  • Quarterly EPS against "expectations" is noise for operating purposes. The direction over several years is what matters.

Cash flow return on invested capital (CFROIC): free cash flow ÷ invested capital

Uncertainty

own calculation Both lines are rough illustrations with year-end capital, which includes acquisitions completed late in 2025 that contributed profit for only part of the year. They are not reported figures and say nothing about how any company measures returns internally. They show the mechanism, not a verdict.

What these lines teach

The capital base is dominated by goodwill and intangibles from acquisitions (about of total assets). An acquirer pays for future profits up front, so its accounting return starts low and must rise as the acquired businesses grow. The businesses themselves need little physical capital: capital expenditure in 2025 was only about of sales.

Value is created when the return exceeds the cost of capital, the return lenders and shareholders require (commonly estimated at high single digits for industrial companies).

The return tree

Return on capital = margin (profit ÷ sales) × capital turnover (sales ÷ capital)

There are two ways to raise it: earn more per dollar of sales, or need less capital per dollar of sales. Modules 3 to 5 covered the first; working capital and capital expenditure are the second.

How to read it

  • The benchmark is the cost of capital, compared like with like. The cost of capital is an after-tax figure, so compare it with the after-tax return (or compare both before tax: 9% after tax corresponds to about 12% before tax at a 25% tax rate). A return above it creates value, below it destroys value, however large the profit.
  • Read the example group's figure with care. About 5% after tax is below an assumed 9%. On the accounting view the acquisitions have not yet earned their cost. That is common in the first years after acquisitions, which is why the trend matters: the return must rise as the acquired businesses grow. The cash-based views, including CFROIC, are higher because amortisation of acquired intangibles is a non-cash charge: the business generates more cash than its accounting profit suggests.
  • Group and plant returns are not comparable. The group carries the price paid for acquisitions; a plant does not. A plant at 34% and a group at 7% can be the same business seen at two levels.
  • Old assets flatter the ratio. A fully depreciated plant shows a high return and may be close to needing replacement. A new plant shows a low return and may be the better business. Cash flow return on investment (CFROI, see the catalogue) corrects for this by measuring cash earned against the original cost of the assets.
  • Average and marginal return differ. The question for a new investment is its own return against the cost of capital, not whether it raises or lowers the unit's average.
  • A return can be raised by starving the business. Cutting investment lifts the ratio for a few years. Check capital expenditure against depreciation.

In your role: operations & supply chain

You influence capital turnover more than anyone: inventory, machine utilisation, and whether new capacity is really needed or existing equipment can deliver more. Higher output from the same assets raises returns twice, through margin and through turnover.

In your role: sales & marketing

A customer with thin margins, long payment terms and consignment stock can have a poor return even at high sales. Look at profit and the capital tied up for a customer together.

In your role: R&D & engineering

A business case is a return calculation: cash out now (development, tooling, launch stock), cash in later. Payback period, net present value and internal rate of return are the same idea applied to one project.

In your role: managers

Ask of every proposal: what capital does it tie up, and what does it earn on that capital compared with the alternatives? Profit growth that needs disproportionate capital can lower the return.

  • Return on capital employed (ROCE): barely changes: $17,500 more profit on $417,000 more capital is about 4%, far below the plant's average and below the cost of capital.
  • Excess and obsolete inventory: the risk grows with every month of stock held; a design change would turn part of the saving into a loss.

1. Customer A asks for a 3% price cut and promises more volume. The contribution margin on this account (price minus variable cost) is 40%. How much more volume makes you whole?

Module 14

Reading the signals: what is really going on?

A single KPI rarely means anything by itself. Interpretation starts when you read several together. Each case shows a pattern; decide what it means before you open the answer. The answer then shows, figure by figure, what each KPI would suggest if you saw it alone and what it contributes to the conclusion, and marks the one that decides the interpretation. Tap a KPI name to open it in the catalogue.

A routine for interpreting any figure

  1. Compared with what? Prior year, plan, peers, the market. A number without a comparison says nothing.
  2. Level or change? A high level and a strong improvement are different findings.
  3. What is it made of? Split it: price, volume and mix; organic and acquired; recurring and one-off.
  4. What moved with it? Check the partner figures: margin with sales, cash with profit, capital with return.
  5. Which decisions caused it? Name them. A figure nobody can link to a decision cannot be managed.
  6. Will it last? Separate what repeats from what was timing, one-off or borrowed from the future.

solid arrow: both move the same way dashed arrow: they move in opposite ways

The symbol on a driver shows which way it has to move to push the selected figure in the chosen direction; the symbol on an effect shows which way it then moves.

The overview shows direction, not size, over the whole chain. Where two routes pull a figure in opposite directions, it shows the usually stronger one and the list names the other as a partial offset; ◆ marks cases that depend on the amounts. Use Lab 1 for the amounts.

Glossary

TermMeaning
AmortisationSpreading the cost of an intangible asset (customer relationships, technology) over its useful life.
Basis point (bp)One hundredth of a percentage point. 500 bps = 5.0 percentage points.
Capital expenditure (capex)Spending on long-lived assets such as machinery and buildings.
Cash conversion cycle (CCC)DSO + DIO − DPO: days between paying suppliers and collecting from customers.
CFROICCash flow return on invested capital: free cash flow relative to the capital provided by lenders and owners.
CFROICash flow return on investment: cash earned by operations relative to the full original cost of the assets in use.
Contribution marginSales minus variable costs: what each additional sale adds to profit while capacity is free.
Cost of capital (WACC)The weighted average return required by lenders and shareholders, after tax; the hurdle for investments.
DepreciationSpreading the cost of a physical asset over its useful life.
EBITEarnings before interest and taxes. In this tutorial the same figure as operating income and operating profit.
EBITDAEarnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation; here calculated as operating income plus depreciation and amortisation.
EPSEarnings per share: net income divided by the number of shares.
Free cash flowCash from operations minus capital expenditure.
GAAPUS Generally Accepted Accounting Principles, the rules for reported figures.
GoodwillThe part of an acquisition price that exceeds the fair value of the identifiable assets acquired.
GuidanceManagement's published forecast for the current year.
MixThe composition of sales by product, customer, channel or region.
Net present value (NPV)Today's value of all future cash flows of an investment minus its cost, discounted at the cost of capital.
NOPATNet operating profit after tax: operating profit less the tax that would be due on it, before interest.
Absorption costingValuing inventory including a share of fixed production overhead. The reason building stock lifts reported profit.
Net leverageNet debt divided by twelve months of EBITDA.
Operating leverageThe effect of fixed costs: profit changes faster than sales.
Organic growthGrowth excluding acquisitions, divestitures and currency translation.
ROICReturn on invested capital: operating profit relative to the capital employed.
SG&ASelling, general and administrative expenses.
Working capitalReceivables plus inventories minus payables: cash tied up in day-to-day operations.
Reference

Sources and method

  • Example group (Modules 1 to 10). Invented: a fictional listed industrial company with two segments. The figures are internally consistent (for example, the bridge in Module 4 sums to the EBITDA shown, and the leverage calculation in Module 7 reproduces the stated ratio) and of a realistic order of magnitude, but they describe no real company.
  • Example plant (Modules 11 to 13). Invented. The plant, its customers and its development project do not exist. The values were chosen to be internally consistent and plausible, not to represent any real site.
  • Own calculations. Ratios marked "own calculation", "own estimate" or "illustration" use simplified textbook formulas. Company-specific definitions often differ in detail.
  • Scope. The tutorial does not describe any company's internal KPI definitions, targets or incentive systems. It is not investment advice.
  • Didactic design (worked examples that fade into independent problem solving): Sweller, J. & Cooper, G. A. (1985). The use of worked examples as a substitute for problem solving in learning algebra. Cognition and Instruction, 2(1), 59–89.
  • Further reading (standard references for the concepts used; cited from memory, check the current edition): Brealey, Myers & Allen, Principles of Corporate Finance (NPV, IRR, cost of capital); Koller, Goedhart & Wessels, Valuation (ROIC, NOPAT, value creation); Horngren, Datar & Rajan, Cost Accounting: A Managerial Emphasis (contribution margin, break-even, absorption costing, flexible budgets, variances).
  • Updating. To use other figures, change the DATA block (example group) or the EX block (example plant) at the top of the script in index.html; tables, charts and calculators recompute automatically. Explanatory sentences that describe causes need a manual review.
Independent learning material. The example group and the example plant are teaching examples; no real company is described. Not investment advice.
